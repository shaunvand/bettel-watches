How it's made

Sixty processes. Thirty of them by hand.

  1. 01

    It began with a sketch

    The first Bettél watches were drawn in pencil on an aeroplane, on the long flight home from Indonesia. No drawing board, no instruments: just the sketches the designs still come from.

  2. 02

    Cutting and shaping the case

    Each case starts as a block of Kiaat, a South African hardwood. It is roughed out by machine, then finished by hand: the holes drilled, the edges rounded, the painting done at the bench.

  3. 03

    The dial

    White beech or American walnut, picked for the way each takes the light, or a disc of carbon fibre set against the grain. Wood being what it is, no two are identical.

  4. 04

    The movement

    Miyota quartz, Japanese, across most of the range, and Japanese self-winding automatics in the Albatross Automatics. Each is set into its case by hand, with tweezers.

  5. 05

    Thirty steps of hand assembly

    Nineteen processes in wood, eleven in leather, thirty in hand assembly. The machine gets the shape close; hands make it a watch.

  6. 06

    Finishing

    Before it leaves the bench, every watch is checked over by eye under a lamp, with cases, dials and straps laid out alongside it.

The first automatic

The movement nobody believed in

This is the first automatic Bettél ever built: a Swiss movement found in an antique shop, written off because nobody believed it would run again. We fixed it and built it into a wooden case, the first of our automatics. Every movement since arrives new.

Our story

No two the same

Every Bettél case is turned from Kiaat, a South African hardwood, sourced locally and chosen by hand. Most of our dials are wood too, white beech or American walnut, picked for the way each takes the light. Wood being what it is, every watch carries its own colour, so no two are identical.

Stuart Swan founded and designs Bettél. He came to watches through furniture and accessories, working with wood long before he made a timepiece from it, drawn to the way a single material can be both structural and beautiful.
